Section 1: Motivation

Why did we build this?

How much housing capacity does SB-79 actually add?

  • If cities want to create their own local plan, they must match the capacity that SB-79 would grant
  • This helps cities determine whether they are in compliance

SB-79 lets cities opt out by adopting a local plan — but that plan must provide at least as much housing capacity as SB-79 would. So to even start that conversation, you need to know the SB-79 baseline. That's what this tool calculates.
Process

How We Built It

To calculate capacity, we first need to know: which parcels get upzoned?

Now let's walk through the pipeline step by step. Each step builds on the last — by the end we'll have every affected parcel tagged with its zone tier and capacity numbers.
Phase 1

We're using Berkeley as our first example. The city boundary comes from the California State Geoportal.

Phase 2

Transit Stops

After getting the city boundary, we map the qualifying transit stops. SB-79 defines Tier 1 and Tier 2 stops — Berkeley only has Tier 1 stops (BART stations).

Crucial Nuance

Calculating Capacity

After this it should be pretty simple, right? We just add up the upzoned capacity?

Not quite. SB-79 includes a clause that incentivizes development on undeveloped land. The capacity increase depends on what already exists on each parcel.

Zone Distance Min. Density
200 ft 0 – 200 feet from stop 160 dwelling units / acre
Quarter Mile 200 ft – 0.25 miles 120 dwelling units / acre
Half Mile 0.25 – 0.5 miles 100 dwelling units / acre
The density numbers look straightforward, but there's a catch. SB-79 says the net increase is the SB-79 capacity minus existing units. So a parking lot gets the full upzone, but a building that already has 100 units gets much less. Let me show you what that looks like.

Here we see two identical 1-acre parcels in the 200ft zone. Parcel A is a parking lot — zero existing units, so the full 160-unit capacity flows through. Parcel B already has 100 units of housing, so SB-79 only adds 60 net new units. This is by design: the law targets underutilized land near transit.

Roadmap

Next Steps

Adding Existing Zoning Capacity

We calculate SB-79 minimums, but what does current zoning already allow? This is complicated:

  • Middle housing zones (R-1, R-2, R-2A, MUR) have a max density of 70 du/acre
  • Higher R & C zones are form-based — capacity = height (stories) × lot coverage

Scaling to New Cities

Each city has its own parcel API, zoning API, and municipal code.

We Need Your Help

  • Can help connect their city's GIS data to the pipeline
  • Know the rules for calculating existing zoning capacity in their city
  • Python/GIS developers familiar with GeoPandas
Two big areas where we need help. First, calculating what's currently allowed — 70 du/acre for middle housing zones with a functional FAR of 1.8 (35ft = 3 stories, 60% lot coverage). The higher zones are form-based. Second, scaling up — every city has different APIs and zoning rules.
